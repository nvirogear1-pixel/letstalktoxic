Prologue

A Small City, A Routine Pull

Cadillac, Michigan. Wexford County. Population approximately 10,000. The municipal wastewater treatment plant is located at 1121 Plett Road and is permitted under NPDES ID MI0020257, discharging to the Clam River watershed (HUC 040601020303).

Under the Clean Water Act, the plant self-reports effluent values monthly on Discharge Monitoring Reports (DMRs). Those values enter EPA's ICIS-NPDES database, where automated screening generates violation records for exceedances.

On September 4, 2026, a review of Michigan permits with cyanide limits identified non-routine rows at this facility.

What follows is the documented record — and the items the record does not establish.

Chapter 2 · The Silence

Four Ways To Explain A Blank Field

A missing violation flag has multiple possible explanations. Each one can be tested against the record:

Refuted — same-DMR proofThe April DMR produced keyed coliform violations (vio_ids 3687723885/86) from the same submission. The June DMR produced a keyed BOD violation (vio_id 3689451305). Same submissions, same screener — the cyanide rows alone received nothing. Not lag.
Refuted — the limit existsCyanide carries an enforceable ENF limit here: 5.9 µg/L monthly average. This is not monitor-only reporting.
Partially testable — systematic, not randomThe cyanide limit appears in the database correctly (5.9 µg/L, ENF type). What differs is a metadata field: the limit's comparison qualifier is coded '=' rather than '≤'. And it is not just cyanide — every water-quality-based toxic limit in this permit version carries the same '=' coding. Systematic, not a typo.
Empirically demonstrated in the tested sampleAcross a 22-permit statewide test, every keyed numeric violation on toxic parameters sat on '≤'-coded limits; every toxic exceedance on '='-coded limits went unflagged. Important: this is an empirical pattern demonstrated in the tested sample — the written ICIS screening rule itself has not been documented by EPA (open gap, #15). No one is accused of anything here; the why of the coding is an adjudication question.

Every flagged numeric violation at this plant 2023–2026 — BOD, coliform, ammonia, phosphorus, dissolved oxygen — sits on a '≤' or '≥' coded limit. Every toxic parameter with '=' coding: arsenic, beryllium, cadmium, copper, silver, selenium, cyanide… none has ever auto-flagged here.

Chapter 5 · The Corridor

Cyanide Was Here Before

Cadillac's industrial past includes a Superfund site a mile from the plant: Northernaire Plating, EPA ID 0502440. A chrome-and-nickel electroplating shop that ran from 1971 to 1981. Its waste handling was, in EPA's eventual judgment, improper — and in 1978, hexavalent chromium turned up in the tap water of two nearby homes.

Here is the fact that earns this chapter a place in the file: the EPA's official contaminant list for the site's groundwater includes cyanide (CAS 57-12-5), alongside the plating solvents — PCE, TCE, the chlorinated ethanes. Standard chemistry for electroplating lines, documented in the 1970s waste streams of this corridor.

Cleanup record: EPA removal action July 5, 1983; 1985 remedial investigation (two small Cr(VI) plumes, shallow and intermediate aquifers); pump-and-treat system installed post-1985; 2010 Five-Year Review finding long-term remedial action goals met; treatment discontinued August 3, 2020 with EPA and EGLE concurrence. No Five-Year Reviews since 2010. EPA's current performance measures: human exposure under control — yes; groundwater migration under control — yes.

FRAMING DISCIPLINE — READ BEFORE CONNECTING DOTS

Nothing in the Northernaire record connects a 40-year-old, EPA-judged-controlled groundwater plume to a 2026 spike in sewer effluent. WWTP effluent reflects current inflow to the pipes, not groundwater under the industrial park. The site is context — proof that cyanide-bearing chemistry has existed in this corridor — and it is presented here only as that. Source attribution is a separate, open question.

How Industrial Wastewater Becomes A Problem

Cyanide doesn't generate itself. The standard industrial pathways are:

  • Metal plating (zinc, cadmium, chrome): Cyanide-based baths are standard chemistry. AAR and Rec Boat both receive plated fasteners and hardware — the question is where that plating happens.
  • Steel foundry operations: Cadillac Castings documents itself as a full-service foundry with scrap-melt charging. Contaminated or pre-plated scrap can introduce metal contaminants into the melt and cooling water.
  • Post-assembly wash/rinse: Even if parts arrive pre-finished, facility wash systems can mobilize plating residue into rinse water headed for the sewer.

Key investigation question: For each facility, does cyanide/metal-bearing wastewater originate from onsite treatment (plating bath, molten metal cooling, wash rinse), or does it arrive in incoming materials (pre-plated fasteners, metal-bearing scrap) and get rinsed/processed onsite?

VERY HIGH INVESTIGATION PRIORITYSupply-chain sourcing

AAR Mobility Systems: Two separate cyanide exceedances (two locations, one year), 47M gallons wastewater. Question: Where do fasteners and plated components originate? Are they arriving pre-finished, or is secondary finishing/coating happening in Cadillac?

Rec Boat Holdings: Documented cyanide exceedance + copper/zinc across all four quarters 2023. Manufactures steel trailers. Question: What is the source of zinc-plated fasteners? Are they finished at a supplier, or does post-assembly treatment occur in Cadillac?

Cadillac Castings Inc.: Multi-element exceedances (Zn, Cd, Ag, Mo) across all of 2023, 41M gallons wastewater. Full-service foundry with scrap charging and CNC finishing. Question: What is the source and contamination profile of incoming scrap/charge material? Are plated or contaminated castings entering the melt?

Prior Enforcement: A 6-Year Compliance Failure

In September 2020, EPA documented that the City's Industrial Pretreatment Program had enforcement failures: more than 15% of significant industrial users were out of compliance simultaneously. The City was directed to update its IPP and strengthen enforcement of local limits.

In February 2022, the City contracted Fleis & VandenBrink (F&V) to visit all 19 permitted facilities, document their processes, and produce an updated IPP. The F&V evaluation reports — including individual facility assessments, process flows, and chemical inventories — should exist in City records.

As of September 4, 2026 (four years later), the updated IPP has not been formally adopted. City Council approved F&V's findings in March 2023, but EGLE is still waiting for the legal authorities and attorney certification required to make local limits enforceable. The December 31, 2026 deadline looms.

What This Means for the April/June 2026 Spikes

The plant experiences SSO/bypass in April 2026 (same month as cyanide peak). EGLE issues NOVs in May and June (contents unknown). Meanwhile, the pretreatment program is still non-compliant: no formal local limits, enforcement incomplete, industrial-user processes not formally documented in force.

This does not prove causation. But it establishes a timing and compliance context: the plant was treating sewage from a documented industrial base with known metal/cyanide exceedances, under a pretreatment program that had not yet been updated to current EPA standards.

NO ACCUSATION — INVESTIGATION FRAMEWORK ONLY

No facility, no supplier, no process is being accused. The question is narrower: which of these operations actually generated the April/June discharge, and did the supply chain originate it onsite or upstream? The answer lives in F&V's evaluation files, facility permits, and supplier documentation.

Why Local Limits Matter (The Mechanism)

Cyanide doesn't move through a WWTP by accident. Local limits are the written enforceable rules that say: "Facility X can discharge no more than Y µg/L of cyanide into the sewer." Without local limits, the City has no mechanism to tell an industrial user their discharge is over the line.

EGLE's mandate in 2020 was clear: update the IPP, establish local limits on toxic metals and cyanide, enforce them. The City contracted F&V to do this work in February 2022. As of August 2026 — four years later — local limits still have not been formally adopted.

The April and June 2026 cyanide spikes occurred during this gap. The City had documented industrial users with known cyanide exceedances (AAR Mobility, Rec Boat) but no formal local limits to enforce against them.

The May 22, 2026 Notice of Violation

On May 22, 2026, EGLE issued the second formal NOV to the City. News articles and citizen-researcher posts tie this to "local limits for toxic pollutants" — the exact mechanism that failed. This is the "second violation notice" referenced in the EPA ECHO record, issued one month after the April cyanide peak.

EGLE was telling the City: your local limits are due. You have industrial users with documented metal/cyanide exceedances. You have no formal mechanism to control their discharge.

The Record: City's Own Statement

Utilities Director Jeff Dietlin defended the delays to news outlets, characterizing the violations as "administrative delays rather than environmental harm" and stating "It's not that we're polluting the river... technical paperwork."

That statement is now preserved — alongside documented cyanide exceedances and the April 16–29 bypass event (16 million gallons of partially treated sewage). The timing overlap is documented. Causation is not claimed.

Cadillac Casting: Monitored User Documented

Cadillac News photo documentation places the city's own Industrial Monitoring Program trailer outside Cadillac Casting collecting samples. Cadillac Casting is a documented monitored industrial user on the City's active inspection record.

Four Winns: Timeline Constraint

Rec Boat Holdings' Four Winns division halted production August 15, 2026 (232 jobs). It was in operation during the April/June spike window — making it a high-priority investigative target for discharge during that period.

TIMING OVERLAP DOCUMENTED — CAUSATION NOT ESTABLISHED

The eight EGLE violations do not directly concern cyanide. But the failure to establish local limits — the exact mechanism governing cyanide discharge — occurred during the spike window. The regulatory backdrop is now clear: the City's pretreatment program was not just incomplete; it was formally cited as noncompliant by the state.
